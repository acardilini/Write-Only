# Encounters

Snapshot of the Encounters tab of the living Claude Doc, 21 November 2026.

A longlist for the walk, drawn from Rothgerber (2020) and the studies it cites. The eight core encounters cover every trigger type and all 15 prevention and reduction outlets in his framework. All 13 encounters (8 core, 5 alternates) are marked **Keep** for testing.

The current word sets for encounters 1–3 live in [`../prototype/garden.json`](../prototype/garden.json). From v0.2, each second-round phrase is linked to specific first-round words; from v0.3, every phrase is written to sound like a person, and encounters with another person ask "What do you say?".

## Design notes for the response paths

1. **Look or walk on.** Before any words, encounters 2, 5, 6 and 7 offer the choice to look or keep walking. Walking on is recorded as avoidance.
2. **Word clouds, not questions.** Words float up and the walker clicks the ones that match their reaction. Nothing asks "did you feel X?", so the mechanism is never named or implied.
3. **Rounds that narrow.** The first round is feelings and reactions, tailored to the encounter. Each pick shapes the next round, which moves towards the thought behind it. If only calm words are picked, the second round offers everyday thoughts and the walk records that prevention held.
4. **Several picks allowed.** Choosing more than one word per round lets co-occurring mechanisms show up naturally.
5. **Words grounded in the literature.** Word sets draw on the language of existing measures, such as the 4N scale (natural, normal, necessary, nice; Piazza et al. 2015, Table 6), the Meat-Eating Justification scale and the Carnism Inventory, adapted rather than copied. Each word's link to a mechanism is labelled with its evidence level.
6. **Neutral exits.** Every round includes "none of these", and word positions are shuffled to avoid order effects.
7. **A behavioural option in every round.** Offering behavioural change before justifications changes the outcome (Rothgerber 2020, §5.3; Tian et al. 2016).
8. **Explicit harm comes late.** Justifications strengthen once practised (§3.3), so encounter 7 comes late and prevention gets a chance to show first.
9. **Sound.** Some encounters use sound, such as the pigs heard inside the truck in encounter 6.
10. **Visual mode matters most at the top.** The illustrated and photographic versions differ most for encounters 6 and 7 (and A4).
11. **Said or thought.** "What do you say?" when another person is present (Smokehouse, vegan stall); "What goes through your head?" when alone. Spoken lines are harder to map to one mechanism, so the end reflection may ask whether what you said matched what you thought.

## Evidence notes from the literature searches (encounters 1–3)

- **Encounter 1:** the key finding is underreporting: when a moral threat was expected, women reported eating less meat than in a control condition (Rothgerber 2019b; not in PubMed, confirmed via Rothgerber 2020). In Piazza et al. (2015) Study 1, "necessary" was the most common reason given for eating meat (36–42%). "It's my choice what I eat" points to meat attachment (entitlement; Graça, Calheiros & Oliveira 2015), outside Rothgerber's framework.
- **Encounter 2:** the pig's head is almost exactly the manipulation in Kunst & Hohle (2016): showing the head reduced dissociation, raised empathy and disgust, and lowered willingness to eat. Effects are larger where unprocessed meat is rarely seen (Kunst & Haugestad 2018). No study yet measures actual behaviour (Benningstad & Kunst 2020), so behavioural options record intention only.
- **Encounter 3:** meeting a vegetarian pushes meat eaters towards denying animal mind, not only towards derogation (Rothgerber 2014a). Morally motivated people inspire rather than irritate when they don't threaten the observer's self-image (Bolderdijk et al. 2018), so the volunteer's manner is a design lever. Resentment of moral rebels: Monin, Sawyer & Marquez (2008). The exact words participants generated in Minson & Monin (2012) were not retrieved; "preachy" and "arrogant" come from secondary sources.
